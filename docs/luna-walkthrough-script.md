# Luna — Voice Walkthrough Script

Tone: first person, conversational, confident — a designer walking a hiring
manager through the work. Approx. 2.5–3 minutes at a natural pace.

---

Hi, I'm glad you're here — let me walk you through Luna.

Luna is a research-driven exploration in human-robot interaction: a social
robot companion for hospitalized in-patients. The problem I started with is
one most of us have seen up close — when someone spends weeks in a hospital
ward, separated from family and routine, loneliness sets in. And that isn't
just an emotional cost. Research shows it degrades mental health and
directly slows recovery.

I framed the problem with a simple 5W breakdown — who, what, where, when,
why — which kept me honest about scope: in-patients, on extended stays, in
the ward itself. My hypothesis was that a social robot could relieve that
isolation — but only if it was designed as a companion, not a gadget.

I set four research objectives: understand how social interaction,
technology, and environment affect loneliness; explore possible
interventions; test feasibility with both patients and healthcare
providers; and isolate what actually drives loneliness when physical
activity is limited.

The process ran from field studies and interviews, through literature
review and competitive analysis, into synthesis and ideation. Two insights
shaped everything that followed. First — social robots must be relational,
not transactional. A robot that only executes commands never becomes a
companion. Second — movement matters. How a robot moves plays a crucial
role in whether people bond with it.

From synthesis, the pain points were clear: limited social interaction,
separation from loved ones, an unfamiliar environment, loss of
independence, and no meaningful activities. I turned those into How Might
We questions — how might we bring family closer, restore independence,
make the environment feel trustworthy — and mapped areas where a robot
could intervene: emotional check-ins, environmental control, entertainment,
guided relaxation, even mimicry to build likeability.

Then I designed Luna itself — success criteria, sketches, and material and
aesthetic choices — and mapped the patient journey from arrival through
daily routine, intellectual stimulation, emotional support, and finally the
transition home. The high-fidelity screens show what that feels like: Luna
greets you by name, catches you up on what you missed — calls from mom,
visitors, messages — offers games and stories, and even adjusts your bed.

I validated the concept using the experience sampling method, and closed
with two honest reflections: conversational design should reach beyond
visual interaction into voice and gesture — and the next step is
accessibility and a smooth handoff to the home after discharge.

That's Luna — thanks for listening. The rest of the case study is right
